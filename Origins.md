Below is a (necessarily finite) map of the main epistemological ways the humble word “sign” has been expanded by philosophers, linguists, scientists and engineers. Each heading names a tradition or discipline and the bullet-points list the key conceptual moves that took the term beyond its everyday meaning of “visible mark”.

1. Ancient “semeion”: the sign as evidence & warrant
Aristotle’s semeion – a material circumstance that licenses an inference (e.g., smoke ⇒ fire).

Early medical writers sharpened the distinction sign vs. symptom: a sign is objectively observable evidence; a symptom is felt by the patient.

In classical rhetoric a semeion functions as indicator or proof, foreshadowing later logical notions of evidential “signs”. 
academia.edu

2. Patristic & Scholastic distinctions: natural vs. conventional signs
Augustine divides signa naturalia (thunder “means” a storm) from signa data (spoken words).

William of Ockham adds mental signs (concepts) and builds a whole epistemology on their “supposition” relations.

Signs become the very medium of ratio (reason), pushing the term into the foundations of medieval theories of knowledge. 
academia.edu

3. Saussurean structuralism: sign = signifier ∕ signified
A sign is an arbitrary pairing of acoustic/graphic signifier with conceptual signified.

Meaning is differential (what a sign is not), giving rise to paradigmatic/syntagmatic analyses and later structural anthropology. 
media-studies.com

4. Peircean pragmatism: triadic sign & infinite semiosis
Representamen – Object – Interpretant: the sign is a relation, not a thing.

Icon / Index / Symbol classify how the sign relates to its object.

Every interpretant can itself be taken as a new sign → knowledge is an open-ended chain of sign production (semiosis). 
plato.stanford.edu

5. Phenomenology & Hermeneutics: sign vs. expression vs. symbol
Husserl distinguishes empty Zeichen (mere indicators) from Ausdruck (expressive signs that intend meaning).

Ricoeur reserves symbol for signs with a “surplus of meaning” that must be interpreted, fueling hermeneutic method.

6. Analytic logic & mathematics: sign as formal symbol
From Frege’s Zeichen (mark) that “expresses a sense and denotes a reference” to Wittgenstein’s rule-governed language-games, signs become the atoms of formal proof.

In computation the sign mutates into the token or bit, detachable from meaning yet indispensable for formal knowledge.

The 1948 model strips meaning away, treating signs as signals quantified in bits.

Later theorists (e.g., Søren Brier’s cybersemiotics) re-introduce the triadic sign to explain why pure syntax is “not enough” for knowledge.

Extends semiosis to cells, genes and ecosystems: chemical messengers, predator markings, even DNA are sign processes.

Knowledge (for an organism) = the successful interpretation of these bio-signs within its Umwelt. 
biosemiotics.org

9. Cybersemiotics & systems theory: sign–information–autopoiesis
Marries Peirce’s triad with second-order cybernetics: systems maintain themselves by producing and interpreting signs that stand in feedback loops with information flows. 
philpapers.org

Halliday / Kress & van Leeuwen treat images, layout, gesture and emoji as semiotic modes that communities negotiate.

In digital culture memes, hashtags and NFTs act as value-laden signs circulating through networked attention economies.

In diagnostics a clinical sign carries epistemic weight precisely because it can be publicly verified.

In the sciences “sign of X” often stands for statistically warranted evidence (a sign of selection pressure, a sign of supersymmetry).

A different bearer – voice, diagram, chemical gradient, byte.

Has the triad “held up”?
Empirical expansion, not abandonment.
Every field that originally flirted with simpler dyads (signal–response in behaviourism, signifier–signified in linguistics, packet–payload in network theory) tends to re-introduce a third entity when explanation bumps into the grounding problem: what turns brute correlations into meaningful correlations. Biosemiotics, multimodal discourse, and cognitive robotics all now talk explicitly about an interpreter layer and its dynamics. 
link.springer.com

Irreducibility defended.
Philosophers such as John Deely and contemporary Peirceans argue the triad is logically irreducible: drop one corner and you smuggle it back in under a new name (e.g., “context” or “user”). 
philpapers.org

Refinements rather than refutations.
Neuroscientists break the interpretant into cascades of predictive-coding layers; AI researchers model multiple “objects” (world-state vs. task-state); media theorists multiply representamina (text, emoji, haptic buzz). Those are elaborations, not disconfirmations, of the original schema.

Where it’s challenged.

Radical enactivism denies any internal representational node, treating cognition as direct skillful coupling; here the triad is regarded as an unnecessary concession to mentalism.

Pure information theory works comfortably with dyads when the goal is merely compression or transmission, not interpretation.

Yet even these programmes concede that explanation of meaningful behaviour—how a signal comes to count as a warning, request or memory—needs at least three relata.

Bottom line: a century after Peirce, the names have proliferated, the corners have gained sub-types, but the core insight—that signification is a tri-relation involving something that stands-for, something stood-for, and some effect in an interpreter—remains the workhorse model wherever researchers care about meaning rather than mere correlation.
